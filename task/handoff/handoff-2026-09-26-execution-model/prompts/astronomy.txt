Explain how astronomers infer the properties of planets orbiting distant stars. Develop the explanation as a connected account for a university student who knows basic mechanics and statistics. Separate the measured quantities from the quantities inferred through a model, and discuss how several independent observations can support or challenge the same conclusion.

Begin with the transit method. Describe the change in stellar brightness as a planet passes in front of a star, the approximate relation between transit depth and the ratio of their radii, and the effects of limb darkening. Explain why a grazing transit can be difficult to interpret and why the duration also depends on orbital geometry. Distinguish measurement noise from uncertainty about the star itself.

Next consider the radial velocity method. A planet and its star orbit their common center of mass, and a spectrograph can measure the periodic motion of the star along the line of sight. Explain the role of orbital inclination and why a radial velocity measurement alone generally gives a minimum planetary mass. Show how combining a transit with a radial velocity signal can reduce this ambiguity without claiming that either instrument directly weighs a planet.

Discuss the importance of repeated observations. A single dip in brightness may be caused by an instrumental disturbance, a stellar companion, or a real planetary transit. Explain how a sequence of events constrains the orbital period, how gaps in sampling create aliases, and why a claim based on a very small number of events must retain alternatives. Give an example in which scheduling the next observation is more informative than merely taking more observations at arbitrary times.

Then explain how stellar activity complicates interpretation. Starspots rotate into and out of view, magnetic regions change the shapes of spectral lines, and stellar oscillations introduce additional variability. Describe how observations in several wavelength bands and indicators of magnetic activity can help distinguish these effects from orbital motion. Explain why a flexible model of correlated noise can both improve an analysis and accidentally absorb a real planetary signal.

Connect the measured mass and radius to the interior of a planet. Explain why bulk density alone does not uniquely determine composition. A rocky core, an ice-rich interior, and a thin hydrogen atmosphere can produce overlapping ranges of radius under different assumptions. Discuss the effects of age, irradiation, and atmospheric escape, and explain why reporting an interval of possible compositions is often more defensible than assigning a single detailed interior structure.

Describe transmission spectroscopy during a transit. Some starlight passes through the upper atmosphere of the planet, and different molecules absorb at different wavelengths. Explain the concept of a scale height and how temperature, gravity, and molecular weight affect the size of the signal. Include clouds, hazes, and contamination by the stellar surface as possible reasons why a spectrum may not have the expected molecular features.

Contrast transmission spectra with thermal emission observations and reflected light. Discuss why the nightside and dayside temperatures need not be equal, what a phase curve can reveal about heat transport, and which assumptions are required to convert a flux measurement into a temperature. Explain why an apparently precise fitted temperature can still depend strongly on the chosen atmospheric model.

Consider a hypothetical system with two transiting planets. Their mutual gravity can shift the times of successive transits. Describe how these timing variations can constrain masses and orbital relationships, why a long observing baseline matters, and why more than one configuration may fit a short time series. Explain how a dynamical stability calculation can reject some solutions while leaving others viable.

Discuss selection effects in a catalog of detected planets. Large planets close to their stars are easier to detect with some methods, whereas other surveys favor massive planets at wider separations. Explain the difference between the distribution of detected planets and the underlying population. Describe injection and recovery experiments and why detection efficiency must be accounted for before estimating how common a class of planets is.

Finally, outline a practical analysis of a newly observed candidate. List the observations that would be most useful, explain how to combine likelihoods without counting the same evidence twice, and distinguish a good prediction of future data from a good fit to existing data. Discuss how the conclusion should change if a new instrument measures a smaller signal than expected. End by describing the evidence needed before claiming that an atmospheric feature indicates a particular chemical process, including plausible alternatives and the value of independent replication.

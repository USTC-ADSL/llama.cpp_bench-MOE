A repair cooperative has received an old mechanical clock that loses several minutes each week. Its owner wants to preserve the original mechanism and avoid replacing parts without a clear reason. One volunteer believes that the problem comes from friction in the gear train, another suspects that the pendulum length changes with temperature, and a third thinks the clock is simply not level. Develop a careful diagnostic procedure that can distinguish these explanations. Start with observations that do not require disassembly, including how to record time error at the same hour each day and how to note changes in room temperature. Explain what evidence would support each hypothesis and what evidence would contradict it. Then describe how a trained technician could inspect lubrication, alignment, wear, and the escapement while keeping a record of the original condition. Discuss why changing several adjustments at once makes diagnosis difficult. The cooperative has limited money and must decide which measurements are worth purchasing instruments for. Offer a practical way to prioritize them, explain the limits of short observation periods, and describe how the owner can judge whether a repair remains successful after the clock returns home. Do not assume that the most expensive intervention is the most effective one.

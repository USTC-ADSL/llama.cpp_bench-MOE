Review a mobile sensor recorder that must retain measurements while its radio is disconnected. Explain which choices affect correctness, which affect energy consumption, and which should be measured rather than assumed. The recorder keeps a monotonic sequence number for every sample and a separate wall clock timestamp. The clock may be corrected after a network connection, but previously assigned sequence numbers must never change. A batch contains readings from several physical sensors, each with its own sampling interval and calibration version. Missing observations are explicitly marked rather than replaced with zero. The server acknowledges a contiguous range of sequence numbers and the device retains all later records until a durable acknowledgement arrives.

The first design uses a fixed size circular log with a checksummed header on each segment. After a restart, recovery scans the segment headers, ignores incomplete records, and resumes from the largest valid sequence number. Discuss a power loss between writing a record and updating the header. Compare storing a committed length in the header against adding a small trailer to every record. A write that appears complete to an application may still reside in a volatile cache, so the ordering of flush operations matters. Tests should interrupt execution at each persistence boundary and compare the reconstructed stream with a reference stream that contains only acknowledged commits.

The upload worker and sampling worker operate concurrently. An upload reads immutable segments and publishes a reclamation watermark after receiving the server acknowledgement. The sampling worker must not overwrite a segment until both the acknowledgement and the local watermark are durable. A retry may transmit the same batch twice. The server therefore deduplicates using device identity and sequence number, not arrival time. Explain how an interrupted acknowledgement can cause harmless retransmission and why it must never cause silent data loss. Consider a device whose battery becomes critically low while the upload connection is open, and propose a policy that finishes a bounded local commit before stopping the radio.

Now consider compression. Slowly varying temperature readings compress differently from vibration data, whose values change quickly. A predictor can store the difference from the previous reading, but restarting within a segment requires a checkpoint of predictor state. Compare short independently decodable blocks with larger blocks that have better compression. Include CPU time, temporary memory, flash writes, and network energy in the discussion. Compression ratio alone does not determine the best choice. A device with a poor radio connection may benefit from more compression, while a device that already has a fast connection may prefer to upload quickly and return to sleep.

Develop a test plan for the recorder. One workload samples several channels at fixed intervals; another introduces bursts after a detected event; a third alternates long disconnected periods with brief uploads. For each workload record queue depth, oldest pending sample age, number of retransmissions, storage occupancy, and battery energy. Report both median delay and long tail delay. Separate time spent waiting for the radio from time spent encoding a batch. Randomly injected faults must be reproducible from a recorded seed, and the reference implementation should verify ordering and absence of dropped committed samples rather than merely count output bytes.

The memory budget is small enough that an unbounded queue is unacceptable. Propose a bounded queue with explicit backpressure and a documented policy for overload. Scientific measurements should not be silently discarded. If the application permits downsampling during overload, record the interval and rule used so that later analysis can distinguish a quiet signal from lost observations. Consider reserving a small emergency segment for status records that describe the overload itself. Explain how reserved capacity changes the worst case storage calculation and why recovery must recognize this segment even if the ordinary data area is full.

Finally, compare two implementations under the same input trace. One uploads every available segment immediately; the other waits for a minimum batch size unless the oldest sample exceeds a deadline. The immediate policy reduces latency but may pay repeated connection setup costs. The batching policy can save energy but risks long delays when traffic is sparse. Define a small online controller using queue occupancy and sample age, then explain how its decisions should be evaluated against both fixed policies. Keep training traces separate from evaluation traces and retain cases where the adaptive policy loses. A useful report includes operational limits, not just the single workload with the largest improvement.

For the final review, summarize the invariants that must hold across reboot, retransmission, clock changes, compression, and reclamation. Trace one sample from acquisition through encoding, persistence, upload, acknowledgement, and eventual storage reuse. At every transition identify who owns the buffer and which event makes reuse safe. Distinguish the cost of copying bytes from the cost of synchronization and durable storage. Explain why avoiding a copy does not automatically eliminate ordering requirements, and why measurements made with a warm cache may fail to predict performance immediately after recovery.
